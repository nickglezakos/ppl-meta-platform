## Purpose

This document outlines a very high-level pricing model for EyeNet in its B2B operating model.

The goal is not to define list prices yet.

The goal is to define:

- how pricing should be structured commercially
- how responsibilities should be separated between software company, distributor, and reseller
- how offers should be positioned for higher-value B2B customers
- how the pricing model can follow established channel patterns similar in spirit to Microsoft or Cisco ecosystems

---

## Core Commercial Principle

EyeNet should be priced as a channel-delivered B2B solution, not as a single public software SKU with one universal price.

The commercial model should be based on three layers:

- the **licensor** sells the software licence and defines product, commercial, and channel policy
- the **distributor** applies channel policy, builds and manages the reseller network, and governs regional or market execution
- the **reseller** owns the end-customer commercial proposal and delivers the project-specific customer contract

This means the final customer offer should usually be customized and solution-based, not a simple public price card.

---

## Proposed Channel Logic

The pricing and agreement structure should follow an established indirect model inspired by mature enterprise software and infrastructure ecosystems.

At a high level:

- the licensor should define partner tiers, software licence rules, branding rules, use policies, and minimum commercial guardrails
- the distributor should manage partner enablement, reseller accreditation, territory or segment execution, and pricing-policy enforcement
- the reseller should package the final customer offer including software, project services, hardware prerequisites, deployment effort, and training

This is closer to a governed channel model than to direct SaaS self-service.

---

## What The Software Company Sells

The software company should sell only the software licence and the right to use the EyeNet products under defined commercial terms.

At a high level, the licensor should own:

- software licensing model
- licence packaging rules
- channel discount structure
- product eligibility and product-bundle rules
- support policy boundaries
- renewal and upgrade policy
- brand and commercial governance

The licensor should not be the normal contracting party for the full end-customer project.

---

## What The Distributor Does Commercially

The distributor should not mainly act as the installation contractor.

The distributor should act as the channel governance and scaling layer.

At a high level, the distributor should:

- apply the licensor's commercial and channel policies
- recruit, structure, and supervise a reseller network
- help standardize offers and commercial practices across the network
- coordinate larger opportunities, strategic customers, or territory-wide programs
- escalate exceptional approvals and non-standard pricing cases
- help maintain pricing discipline in the market

The distributor is therefore a policy and network multiplier, not just a resale intermediary.

---

## What The Reseller Sells

The reseller should own the end-customer contract.

That contract should normally include more than software.

At a high level, the reseller offer may include:

- software licence as part of the full solution
- presales discovery and opportunity qualification
- customized study of customer use cases
- project scoping and deployment design
- assessment of hardware prerequisites such as PCs, laptops, tablets, cameras, displays, networking, or edge devices
- implementation services
- hardware and software installation
- configuration and deployment support
- customer training
- post-go-live support coordination

This means the final customer price should be solution-led and project-led, not only licence-led.

---

## Pricing Model Recommendation

The pricing model should be structured in layers.

### 1. Software Licence Layer

This is the amount charged by the licensor into the channel.

At a high level this should be based on factors such as:

- product family
- deployment mode
- installation size
- camera count or monitored points
- use-case complexity
- edition or bundle level
- annual term or multi-year term

This layer should remain the licensor-controlled foundation.

### 2. Channel Commercial Layer

This is the channel margin and commercial policy layer applied through distributor and reseller structure.

At a high level this should cover:

- distributor margin
- reseller margin
- deal-registration or protected-opportunity logic
- strategic-account approvals
- non-standard discount approvals
- partner-tier based commercial treatment

This layer should resemble enterprise channel practices from established software vendors rather than ad hoc project pricing.

### 3. Project Services Layer

This is the reseller-led project and implementation layer.

At a high level this should cover:

- site study
- use-case analysis
- deployment design
- installation effort
- system configuration
- hardware integration
- training
- rollout support
- optional ongoing managed services

This layer will vary by customer and is one of the main reasons the final commercial offer should be customized.

### 4. Hardware Layer

Hardware should generally be treated separately from the software licence.

At a high level this may include:

- cameras
- laptops or PCs
- tablets
- displays
- local servers or edge computers
- smart glasses and related equipment for future products such as Security Officer Agent
- networking and accessories

This should usually be priced and contracted by the reseller as part of the project solution.

## Product Packaging Direction

Based on the current product roadmap, pricing should likely be organized by product family and deployment profile rather than by one universal platform fee.

At a high level the pricing outline should accommodate products such as:

- Intelligent Signage
- Underage Detector
- Gate Activity
- Room & Gate Protection
- Sentinel
- Security Officer Agent

A simple future structure could evolve around:

- single-product offers
- vertical bundles
- multi-site enterprise bundles
- fixed-installation packages
- mobile-operation packages
- premium security or compliance packages

This keeps pricing aligned with how customers actually buy solutions.

---

## Customer Offer Philosophy

For high-value B2B customers, the commercial offer should normally be customized.

The customized offer should reflect:

- the operational use case
- the number of sites or monitored zones
- the deployment mode
- the hardware environment
- implementation complexity
- training requirements
- service expectations
- customer scale and strategic importance

This means EyeNet should avoid positioning itself primarily as a public-price SaaS catalogue in these channel-led cases.

---

## Agreement Structure Direction

The agreement stack should follow the established three-level enterprise channel pattern.

At a high level:

- **Licensor-Distributor Agreement**: defines licence rights, channel obligations, commercial policy, territory or market rules, branding, reporting, and governance
- **Distributor-Reseller Agreement**: defines reseller enablement, sales rights, margin structure, channel obligations, service expectations, and policy compliance
- **Reseller-End Customer Agreement**: defines the final customer solution, commercial terms, implementation scope, hardware scope, services, support handling, and deployment obligations

This keeps software ownership, channel governance, and project delivery clearly separated.

---

## Recommended High-Level Pricing Position

The recommended positioning is:

- EyeNet is a **licensed software platform sold through the channel**
- distributors and resellers do **not** merely pass through a licence; they commercialize and operationalize the solution
- the end-customer price is a **customized offer** built around software, deployment, hardware, training, and project services
- the software company should preserve control of licence logic and channel policy, while the reseller preserves control of the customer project contract

---

## What Should Come Next In Later Versions

Future versions can refine this high-level model into:

- partner tier definitions
- licence metrics
- bundle logic by product line
- renewal rules
- support packaging
- approval thresholds for discounting
- margin guardrails
- deal registration policy
- strategic account exceptions
- sample offer structures for small, medium, and enterprise customers

For now, the important business position is simple:

EyeNet should be sold as a channel-governed software licensing business with reseller-led customized customer offers, not as a one-size-fits-all publicly priced software product.
